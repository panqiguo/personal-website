On His Team, Would You Be a Solvent, or the Glue?  
在他的团队里，你会是溶剂，还是胶水？

June 12, 2009  
2009年6月12日

This interview of the video game designer Will Wright was conducted and condensed by Adam Bryant.  
这篇对电子游戏设计师威尔·莱特（Will Wright）的访谈由亚当·布莱恩特（Adam Bryant）采访并整理。

Q. How do you give feedback to the people you manage?  
问：你如何向自己管理的人提供反馈？

A. A lot of the people I’ve managed — artists, programmers, producers — they don’t want to know just if they are doing a good job or not. They want to be pushed and challenged in their career.  
答：我管理过的许多人——艺术家、程序员、制作人——并不只是想知道自己做得好不好。他们希望自己的职业生涯能受到推动和挑战。

So, if they feel like you are presenting things to them in such a way that, a year later, they are definitely going to be a better artist or a better programmer, then it really feels like a win-win. Even if you give them tough critical feedback, they see the benefit and value of it, as opposed to just a typical performance review.  
所以，如果他们觉得你是在以这样一种方式和他们沟通：一年之后，他们肯定会成为更好的艺术家或程序员，那么这件事就真的会变成双赢。即使你给出的是严厉的批评性反馈，他们也能看到其中的好处和价值，而不会把它仅仅视作一次普通的绩效考核。

Q. How did you come to appreciate the difference?  
问：你是怎样意识到这种差别的？

A. For a lot of people, their job and their position are not the relevant part of how they see themselves. They have an internal view of themselves, their career aspirations, the direction they want to go. The really important motivational stuff is more in their secret identity.  
答：对许多人来说，工作和职位并不是他们看待自己的关键部分。他们内心里有一套对自己的认识，有自己的职业抱负，也有自己想去的方向。真正重要的激励，更多存在于他们隐藏的身份之中。

Q. And how do you get a sense of that?  
问：那你怎样了解这一点？

A. A lot of that has to do with talking to them. You want to spend a fair amount of time exploring their interests, what they do outside of work. Usually people always have some passion that really drives them.  
答：很大一部分要靠和他们交谈。你需要花相当多的时间去了解他们的兴趣，以及他们在工作之外做些什么。通常每个人都会有某种真正驱动着他们的热情。

And this to me is one of the important points of working collaboratively with other people — trying to get a sense of what is the one thing that makes their eyes light up, they get excited about and they won’t stop talking about. And if you can get a sense of what that is from somebody, and you can harness that, that’s going to have more impact on how they perform their job, how they relate to you, how you can convey a vision to them in a way that they get excited about it.  
对我来说，这也是与人协作时一个很重要的要点——试着弄清楚，到底是什么事情会让他们眼睛发亮，让他们兴奋，让他们说个不停。只要你能从某个人身上感受到这一点，并且能够善用它，它对他们如何完成工作、如何与你相处，以及你如何以一种能让他们兴奋的方式向他们传达愿景，都会产生更大的影响。

For me that’s the real key to a lot of this stuff — exploring and understanding the personal passions that people working with you have.  
对我而言，这才是很多事情真正的关键——去探索并理解那些和你一起工作的人所拥有的个人热情。

Q. And how did you learn to do that?  
问：那你是怎样学会这么做的？

A. I initially really noticed this in myself. If I was working on something I was really into, nothing was going to get in my way, no matter what obstacles came up in front of me. If it was a task that somebody gave me and I wasn’t that into it, it was more like you were just sitting there turning the crank and going through the motions.  
答：起初，我是在自己身上真正注意到这一点的。如果我正在做一件自己非常投入的事，无论前面有什么障碍，都没有什么能挡住我。但如果那只是别人交给我的任务，而我并没有那么感兴趣，那就更像是坐在那里机械地摇着把手，按部就班地完成动作。

So I started realizing that the more I could apply my passions to my work, the leverage was tremendous, and it was an amazing amplifier. Everybody is like that, in some sense — they have things that really drive them to do extraordinary work.  
于是我开始意识到，越能把自己的热情投入到工作中，产生的杠杆效应就越惊人，它是一种了不起的放大器。从某种意义上说，每个人都是这样——他们都有一些东西，能够真正驱使他们做出非凡的工作。

Q. You are in the business of creating games that probably sound close to impossible when you first explain your ideas. How do you get your team on board?  
问：你从事的是这样一种工作：当你第一次解释你的游戏创意时，它们听起来往往近乎不可能实现。你如何让团队接受并投入其中？

A. A lot of it comes down to picking the right people. Because, when you come in with an idea like that, that sounds impossible, some people will be very excited, while others might say: “Oh no, we’re doomed. I want a different project. I want a transfer.” So, that’s part of how I filter prospective employees — when they are faced with the impossible, do they get excited or do they get depressed?  
答：很大程度上，这取决于你是否选对了人。因为，当你带着一个听起来不可能实现的想法出现时，有些人会非常兴奋，另一些人则可能会说：“噢不，我们完了。我想换个项目，我想调走。”所以，这也是我筛选潜在员工的一部分标准——当他们面对不可能的事时，是会兴奋，还是会沮丧？

Q. How do you make sure you have the right people?  
问：你怎样确保自己找到了合适的人？

A. When you are building a team, there is a balance issue. There are seasoned veterans I’ve worked with 10 or 15 years. And then you have these young kids coming out of graduate courses who are very motivated and they’re not set in their ways. And they actually work very well together. And we’ll have another third that is kind of an eclectic mixture.  
答：组建团队时，关键在于平衡。有些是我共事了十年或十五年的资深老手。也有一些是刚从研究生课程毕业的年轻人，他们很有动力，也还没有形成固化的做事方式。而且他们其实能很好地协同工作。团队里还有另外三分之一左右的人，则是一种多元混合的组合。

I think there is a lot of value in diversity. Some of the early games we did tended to appeal a little bit more to women than most other games. So we started getting the highest-qualified women in the game industry coming to us first. So there’s a gender diversity and there’s an age diversity. If you look at a lot of game companies, it’s primarily 25-year-old guys working in them.  
我认为多样性很有价值。我们早期做的一些游戏，比大多数其他游戏更容易吸引女性玩家。所以，游戏行业中资质最优秀的一批女性开始优先来找我们。于是团队里有性别多样性，也有年龄多样性。看看许多游戏公司，里面工作的主要都是二十五岁左右的男性。

Q. And when you are interviewing an intern, what are you asking?  
问：面试实习生时，你会问些什么？

A. Interns always have some vision that they want to fulfill, and it really gives you an insight into what their passions are. Sometimes it will be a very unformulated idea; other times it’s something that they have really spent a lot of time and effort developing. That’s a little bit different than their qualifications and their competencies.  
答：实习生总会有某种他们想要实现的愿景，而这会让你真正看见他们的热情所在。有时那只是一个尚未成形的想法；有时则是他们已经投入大量时间和精力去发展的东西。这和他们的资历、能力是稍有不同的。

The next step is, do they have the ability to get there, and, also, how open are they to understanding the things they don’t know and wanting to learn about that.  
下一步要看的是，他们是否具备实现它的能力；以及，他们对于理解自己尚不了解的事物、愿意去学习这些东西，到底有多开放。

Q. Your teams inevitably hit a lot of dead ends when they’re creating a game. How do you deal with that?  
问：团队在制作游戏时，难免会撞上许多死路。你如何处理这种情况？

A. When I’m managing creative people, the way they relate to failure is very important. Because there are certain types of failure that you really want to celebrate. I personally learned a lot more from my failures than from my successes. And if you look at it that way, then all my failures, you know, in some sense brought me to my larger successes, because I recognized why I failed, and I learned from it. And so, at that point, you can even argue that it’s not a failure. It’s part of your learning process.  
==答：当我管理创意人员时，他们如何看待失败非常重要。因为有些类型的失败，其实是你真心应该庆祝的。我个人从失败中学到的东西，比从成功中学到的多得多。换个角度看，某种意义上，我所有的失败都把我带向了更大的成功，因为我认识到了自己为何失败，并且从中学习。到了那个时候，你甚至可以说，它已经不算失败了。它是学习过程的一部分。

And so, even with interns, it’s kind of interesting to see how they relate to failure. Does it motivate them, do they go a different direction, do they give up or do they learn from it and get some insight and add it as part of their tool chest? In some sense it is an award that they’ve earned.  
所以，即使面对实习生，观察他们如何对待失败也很有意思。失败会激励他们吗？他们会转向另一条路吗？他们会放弃吗？还是会从中学习，获得一些洞见，并把它加入自己的工具箱？从某种意义上说，这是一种他们赢得的奖赏。

One of the questions I will usually ask somebody when I am interviewing them is, what was your biggest failure? And what did you learn from it and what would you have done differently? Within a team setting, a lot of times we’ll go down paths and we’ll prototype things. And at some point we’ll realize it was a bad branch and we have to back up and go take a different branch. Those forays — as a team, we can celebrate those.  
面试时，我通常会问一个问题：你最大的失败是什么？你从中学到了什么？如果重来一次，你会怎么做得不同？在团队工作中，我们常常会沿着某条路径走下去，做出原型。到了某个时刻，我们会意识到那是一条错误的分支，必须退回来，走向另一条路。作为一个团队，我们可以庆祝那些探索。

Q. What about interviewing industry veterans?  
问：那么面试行业资深人士时呢？

A. For somebody like that, it’s more a matter of getting a sense of how clear are they on where they want to go relative to where they’ve been. And there is the matter of, how good is this person, times their teamwork factor. You can have a great person who doesn’t really work well on the team, and they’re a net loss. You can have somebody who is not that great, but they are really very good glue, so that could be a net gain.  
答：对于这样的人，更多是要了解：相对于他们已经走过的路，他们对自己接下来想去哪里到底有多清楚。还有一个问题是，这个人有多优秀，乘以他们的团队协作系数。你可以有一个非常厉害的人，但他并不真正适合团队合作，那他总体上就是一种净损失。你也可以有一个没那么出色的人，但他非常擅长把团队黏合在一起，那么他就可能带来净收益。

A lot of team members I consider glue within the team in that they disseminate things effectively, they motivate and improve the morale of people around them. They basically bring the team tighter and tighter.  
我认为，许多团队成员在团队中就是“胶水”：他们能有效地传递信息，激励身边的人，提高周围人的士气。他们本质上是在让团队变得越来越紧密。

Other ones are solvents and, it’s just their kind of personal nature that they might be disagreeable. They rub people the wrong way. They’re always caught in conflicts. But, for the most part, that is as least as important as their competence in their role.  
另一些人则是“溶剂”。这只是他们个人性格的一部分：他们可能不太讨人喜欢，容易让人不舒服，总是卷入冲突之中。但在大多数情况下，这一点至少和他们在岗位上的能力同样重要。

Occasionally I will get somebody who is more of a prima donna, who is just incredibly good, but not great on the team and so, in some ways you can find a role where you can kind of isolate and quarantine them and allow them to go off and do their great work without having to interact with the rest of the team a lot. Those people are fairly few and far between.  
偶尔我也会遇到那种更像“明星人物”的人：他们确实极其出色，但在团队中表现并不好。某种意义上，你可以给他们找一个能够相对隔离的位置，让他们去做自己擅长的出色工作，而不必与团队其他成员频繁互动。不过这样的人相当少见。

Q. How do you glean that from an interview?  
问：你如何从面试中看出这些？

A. That part is very hard to get from an interview. A lot of times you can subtly kind of push back on things they are saying and find out if they are argumentative, or do they tend to take the conversation in a constructive direction. Their self-image relative to what other people have said about them is really interesting to me. When you call their references, if there is a very big discrepancy between their self-image and what other people are saying about them, that is usually indicative of some underlying kind of social issue you are going to be facing down the road.  
答：这一部分很难仅凭面试看出来。很多时候，你可以对他们说的话稍微提出一点反驳，看看他们是否好争辩，还是倾向于把谈话带向建设性的方向。他们的自我形象，与其他人对他们的评价之间的关系，对我来说很有意思。当你联系他们的推荐人时，如果他们对自己的看法，和其他人对他们的评价之间存在非常大的差距，通常就意味着未来你可能会面对某种潜在的社交问题。

On the other hand, you know, if they come and they are underselling themselves and you talk to everybody they worked with and they are telling you they’re a superstar, then unusually that indicates that they are going to an added benefit in the team setting.  
另一方面，如果他们来面试时低估自己，而你和所有曾与他们共事的人交谈后，大家却都说他们是超级明星，那么这通常意味着，他们在团队环境中会带来额外的价值。

Q. Do you find yourself focusing on certain things more in prospective hires for your team than you did, say, 10 years ago?  
问：和十年前相比，你现在在考察团队候选人时，会更关注某些特定的事情吗？

A. I would say I’m looking more and more for autonomy in my hires. I look continually for more and more points of leverage where I can manage creative staff without a huge amount of bandwidth required.  
答：我会说，我越来越看重候选人的自主性。我一直在寻找更多的杠杆点，让我能够在不占用大量精力和沟通带宽的情况下管理创意人员。

One of the things that I have done with people that I work with is, they will always come to me for a decision: “Do, you think we should do A or B?” More and more I will ask them to guess which one we would pick. They’ll say, “Well, you know, we were thinking you would pick A.” And then you give them feedback. “Hey, you’re right: I would have picked A,” or “No, you’re wrong: I would have picked B, and this is why.” And the point of that really is to get them to develop an internal model of you.  
我和共事的人做过一件事：他们总会来找我做决定：“你觉得我们该选 A 还是 B？”而我越来越常做的是，让他们先猜我们会选哪一个。他们会说：“嗯，我们觉得你会选 A。”然后你再给他们反馈：“对，你猜对了，我会选 A。”或者：“不，你猜错了，我会选 B，原因是这个。”这样做真正的目的，是让他们在内心里建立起一个关于你的模型。

Over time you basically want them to come to you with fewer and fewer of those decisions. And this is how you push them out of the nest.  
随着时间推移，你基本上会希望他们越来越少带着这类决定来找你。这就是你把他们推出巢穴的方式。

Q. How do you manage your time?  
问：你如何管理时间？

A. We’ve tried a lot of different things. There was a point a few years ago whenever somebody called a meeting that I had to attend, then whoever called the meeting had to pay me a dollar. And I got a lot of dollars that way. It did make them think twice about calling the meeting, even though it was only a dollar.  
答：我们试过很多不同的方法。几年前有一段时间，只要有人召集一个我必须参加的会议，召集人就得付我一美元。那样我赚到了很多美元。尽管只是一美元，但这确实会让他们在发起会议前多想一遍。

We would also invite one of my lead artists, a guy named Ocean Quigley, to these general creative meetings. He gets very impatient when things go off track and are not relevant to him, and I found that Ocean was like the canary in the coal mine. Whenever the meeting started getting off track, Ocean would usually raise his hand and say something like, “Oh, do you need me in the meeting anymore?’ And then he would try and get out of the meeting. And so, that was the point at which we always knew the meeting had hit diminishing returns, because our canary had put up his hand.  
我们还会邀请我的一位首席艺术家——一个叫奥申·奎格利（Ocean Quigley）的人——参加这些一般性的创意会议。当事情跑题、与他无关时，他会变得很不耐烦。我发现，奥申就像煤矿里的金丝雀。每当会议开始偏离主题时，他通常会举起手，说些类似的话：“哦，这个会议还需要我吗？”然后他就会试图离开会议。所以，那就是我们总能知道会议已经进入边际收益递减阶段的时刻，因为我们的“金丝雀”举手了。

It’s more of a philosophy, you know, we just try to convey to everybody on the team. It’s almost more like you want to encourage everybody to be a hall monitor. “Hey, are we wasting our time here, or not?”  
这更像是一种理念，我们只是试图把它传达给团队里的每一个人。它有点像是鼓励每个人都充当走廊监督员：“喂，我们现在是不是在浪费时间？”

Q. So people will actually say that?  
问：所以人们真的会直接这么说？

A. Oh, yeah. We try to give positive feedback: “Oh, you’re right, you know, we’re wasting time here. You five can leave.” Not making it taboo is the main thing. It is purely a cost-benefit issue, right? It’s nothing personal.  
答：当然。我们会尽量给出正向反馈：“哦，你说得对，我们确实在这里浪费时间。你们五个人可以离开了。”最重要的是，不要把这件事变成禁忌。这纯粹是一个成本收益问题，对吧？没有任何私人因素。

Q. What’s your two-minute commencement speech?  
问：如果让你做一场两分钟的毕业演讲，你会说什么？

A. I would first of all talk about the value of failure, because I think everybody’s leaving school kind of with a mind-set that, “Oh, I’m going out and I have to succeed. You have to succeed.” And if they hit a failure it has the potential to, you know, de-motivate them, and push them in a bad direction. But, if they can embrace and celebrate their failure, it kind of gives them a totally different outlook on what they are doing.  
==答：我首先会谈失败的价值，因为我觉得每个人离开学校时，都会带着这样一种心态：“哦，我要走入社会了，我必须成功。你必须成功。”而一旦他们遭遇失败，失败就可能让他们失去动力，把他们推向不好的方向。但如果他们能够接纳甚至庆祝自己的失败，这会让他们对自己正在做的事拥有完全不同的看法。

I think also the way the world is changing today, particularly when somebody leaves with a degree and they then go look for a work spot where they can really, you know, fit in: “This situation fits me very well.” And I think I would encourage them — rather than try to fit in somewhere — to find someplace where they can craft the environment, the job and the situation — basically, make it fit you.  
==我也会谈到，当今世界正在发生的变化。尤其是，当一个人拿着学位离开学校，然后去寻找一个自己能够真正融入的工作位置：“这个环境很适合我。”我想鼓励他们，与其试图融入某个地方，不如去寻找一个你能够塑造环境、工作和处境的地方——本质上，是让它来适合你。
